A traced outline says there is a window here. A depth scan says how far back the glass sits, which way the reveal faces, and where the sill stands proud of the brick — and that turns out to be enough to find the windows without anybody drawing them, and enough to light the house rather than paint it.

00

What a scan buys

Live · nothing uploaded

Everything on this page is computed in your browser, right now, by the same two modules the app would use. The house below is synthetic — a heightfield of a bay-fronted terrace, triangulated, rotated to face an arbitrary direction and dropped forty metres from the origin, exactly as a phone scan arrives. Nothing downstream is told where the wall is, how big it is, or that it has windows.

No texture, no hand-built model, no artwork. Every pixel of that light is N·L against a surface normal differentiated from the scan, with the shadows ray-marched through the same heightfield. The reveals darken on one side and catch on the other because the reveals are really there.

01

Find the wall

RANSAC plane fit

A scan is a million points in a coordinate system nobody chose. The first question is which plane has the most points on it, because on the front of a house that plane is the front of the house. Answer that and you have the wall's pose in metres — the one fact a photograph cannot give you, and the fact the app currently asks you to supply by marking a rectangle and typing its real size.

02

Relief, not depth

Orthographic · metric

Depth from the camera is mostly a statement about where you were standing. The useful quantity is relief: how far each point stands in front of or behind the wall's own plane. Rasterise the mesh orthographically along that plane's normal and you get a picture that is square on the building and square in millimetres, in which a window is not a shape — it is the part of the wall that is a hundred millimetres further away.

set back gradient standing proud

Twenty millimetres is the whole judgement call. It clears rendered brickwork, pebbledash and scan noise, and sits comfortably under the fifty to a hundred a window reveal gives you. Drag it and watch the brick joints arrive at about six.

03

The shapes trace themselves

Label · trace · square off

Label the connected regions, walk the boundary along the cracks between pixels, and simplify. Openings are rectangles, so a region that genuinely fills its own bounding box is squared off rather than recorded faithfully as a wobble. Then the measurements are in metres, which is what makes the tags possible at all: a door is not "a tall rectangle", it is two metres high and standing on the ground.

The bay comes back as one region tagged wall, rather than as a bay with three windows in it. That is correct as far as it goes — every part of it does stand proud of the brickwork — and it is the limit of fitting one plane: those panes are set back into the bay, not into the house. Fitting a second plane inside each large region would find them, and is the obvious next thing to try.

04

Why not just edge-detect the photograph

The honest comparison

Because a photograph is a picture of the paint. Below is the same facade as a daylight still, with the things a real one has: brick courses, a patch of ivy over one window, a downpipe, the shadow under the eaves, and one window painted the same colour as the wall. On the left, a Sobel operator does exactly what it is asked. On the right, the same regions from relief.

The ivy is the interesting failure. It is a real edge, it is where the window is, and it is not the window — an outline traced from it would light a leaf. Depth does not see paint, shadow, moss or bunting, which is also why this keeps working after dark, when the app is actually running.

05

Then the light knows where the house is

Normals · shadows

Relief differentiates into a surface normal per pixel, and a normal is all a lighting model wants. That is the part I would not have guessed was worth the trouble until I saw it: the effects library is already careful to model light rather than paint, and this hands it the missing half — a real surface to fall on. A lantern carried past the house lights the reveals from the side. Lightning throws the porch's shadow across the path. Fire at the foot of the wall catches the underside of every sill and nothing else.

07

What is real and what is not

Plainly
  • Built and tested. The plane fit, the orthographic relief bake, hole filling, normals, region tracing and the opening detection, plus a glTF reader that handles node transforms and strided accessors. Two test files drive them end to end against a synthetic house at an arbitrary pose.
  • Not yet wired into the app. Nothing here writes shapes into a project, and no effect can read the relief map yet. That is the next piece of work, and it is mostly plumbing: store the relief beside the still, align it to the camera view with the four-point tool that already exists, and hand it to effects the way the camera frame is handed to them today.
  • The alignment is the real question. Openings come out in wall coordinates, in metres — the same space the app builds when you square up a wall. Getting from there to the camera's view is one homography, so it is four clicks at worst, and free if you have already squared the wall.
  • Depth photos will disappoint you. Portrait-mode depth is tuned for a face at arm's length and saturates well before the far side of a front garden; the LiDAR sensor gives up around five metres. Photogrammetry from a walk-around is the mode that works on a whole house, and it is also the one that needs no special sensor.
